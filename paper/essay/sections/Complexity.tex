\section{Complexity analysis}\label{sec:complexity}

Filling the table is $\Theta(nm)$ in time and $\Theta(nm)$ in space. Computing the length alone is
still $\Theta(nm)$ in time but only $\Theta(m)$ in space. For the two genomes that is
$8.9 \times 10^8$ cells: 3.6 GB as a table against 239 KB as two rows of 32-bit integers.


At the top level the two last-row computations cost $nm$ cell updates. At the next level the two
sub-rectangles have total area at most $nm / 2$, because the split cuts both $X$ and $Y$. The areas
form a geometric series, so the total is at most $2nm$ cell updates, that is, $O(nm)$ time with
$O(n + m)$ memory, and the depth is $O(\log n)$. The constant is worth stating: about twice the work
of the length-only version, which is what the measurements show, 3.79 s against 1.68 s on the full
genomes in the benchmark run (Table~\ref{tab:complexity}).

Naive search is $O(nm)$ in the worst case, reached by the adversarial text $A^n$ with the pattern
$A^{m-1}C$, where every alignment compares all $m$ symbols. On real DNA a mismatch occurs after about
1.3 comparisons on average, the expected value for a four-letter alphabet, so naive search behaves
linearly. KMP is $O(m)$ preprocessing plus an $O(n)$ scan, because the text pointer only advances and
the pattern pointer decreases at most as often as it increases. The Z-function is $O(n + m)$ by the
same amortised argument applied to the right boundary.

Greedy ORF selection costs $O(n)$ to scan the three frames and $O(c \log c)$ to sort the $c$
candidates, against $O(2^c)$ for the exhaustive subset search, whose doubling per candidate is
visible in Figure~\ref{fig:search_space}. The worst case of backtracking is the number of encodings,
$\prod_{t \le k} \deg(a_t) \le 6^k$, but each node costs only the size of its candidate set, and
pruning keeps the explored nodes near-constant in $k$ once the prefix is specific enough. Branch and
bound has the same $O(4^k \cdot n)$ worst case as exhaustive search. The measured benefit is on nodes
and not on time, because the bound $|\text{alive}|$ is loose at shallow depth, where most positions
are still alive and most of the filtering work happens.

Centre expansion is $O(n^2)$ in the worst case, with a measured slope of 2.02 on $A^n$, and
Manacher is $O(n)$, with a slope of 1.04 on the same input. On real DNA both are effectively linear,
at slopes 1.04 and 1.02, because palindromes are short. Manacher's advantage therefore only shows on
adversarial input.
