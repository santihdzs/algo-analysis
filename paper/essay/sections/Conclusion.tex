\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

Dynamic programming gives the exact answer, but its table is the bottleneck. Divide and conquer did
not change the time bound, it changed what fits in memory, and that is what made the full-genome
comparison possible. The linear-time string algorithms only pay off when the pattern is repetitive:
on real DNA brute force is already linear in practice, so the theoretical worst case is what
justifies KMP, not the average run. Greedy is optimal for the objective it is given, and the
ORF experiment shows that the objective is the whole story, since maximum count and maximum coverage
are different problems. Backtracking turns an exponential enumeration into a few dozen nodes because
the genome is a very strong constraint, and its one apparent failure was the frameshift, a fact about
the data. Branch and bound is only as good as its bound: the same optimum with far fewer nodes, but
no time saved, because the bound is loose where the work is. Manacher is the only technique whose
advantage was invisible on the real data and visible only under an adversarial input.

Comparing the techniques, the linear string algorithms and greedy scaled best, while the LCS and the exhaustive
baselines needed the most resources. The datasets changed the behaviour: real DNA against adversarial
input flips both the naive-versus-KMP and the centre-versus-Manacher comparisons, and the length
threshold flips the greedy result. Hirschberg is the best approach here, on one criterion: it is the
only technique that made an infeasible problem feasible, turning a 3.6 GB table into linear space and
so allowing the two genomes to be compared at all. Backtracking had the largest gain over its
baseline, 108 nodes against 995\,328 encodings at $k = 10$, but its baseline could still be run.

Relating experiment to theory worked in three ways. The log-log slopes reproduce the exponents of
the complexity bounds, so the measurements confirm the analysis instead of replacing it. Constants
matter as well as exponents: numpy against pure Python, and the $2nm$ of Hirschberg against the $nm$
of the length-only row. And the baseline has to be run to know whether the sophisticated algorithm
was needed at all.
